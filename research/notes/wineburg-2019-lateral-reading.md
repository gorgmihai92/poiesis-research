# Lateral Reading and the Nature of Expertise: Reading Less and Learning More When Evaluating Digital Information (Wineburg & McGrew, 2019)

- **Source:** Teachers College Record 121(11): 1–40 · https://doi.org/10.1177/016146811912101102
- **File:** none (reference only)
- **Licence:** © SAGE: closed.
- **Evidence:** empirical (expert/novice study; 45 participants: 10 historians, 10 professional fact checkers, 25
  undergraduates) · **abstract only**.

## What it says (from the abstract)
- Historians and students often fell for easily manipulated features of websites, such as official-looking logos
  and domain names, and read vertically, staying within a site to judge it.
- Fact checkers read laterally: they left a site after a quick scan and opened new tabs to judge its credibility,
  and reached more warranted conclusions in a fraction of the time.

## What it means for the plugin
- Before relying on a non-academic source, find out who is behind it from other sources, not from the source's own
  pages; name the publisher and its interest next to the claim. Small sample: a method that works for experts, not
  a measured effect size.
